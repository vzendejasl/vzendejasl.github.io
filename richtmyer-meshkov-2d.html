2D calculation

Richtmyer–Meshkov instability with reshock, with AMR

A shock crosses a perturbed interface between two fluids, the interface rolls up into spikes and bubbles, and a second shock later hits the mixing layer again as it breaks down into fine-scale mixing.

Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) refines the mesh during the run where the flow is changing. Here it follows the mixing of the fluids and the pressure gradients at the shocks. That can cut the computational cost, but post-processing gets more complicated, on top of the code development AMR needs.

The first movie below shows the volume fraction of the two fluids in the top panel and the adaptive mesh in the bottom panel. The second movie is the same calculation without the mesh, with density gradients overlaid on the volume fraction. Where the vorticity comes from is explained further down the page.

These are high-order finite element (FEM) calculations, so the mesh is counted in elements. In Q2Q1 the velocity and the mesh are continuous quadratic (Q2) elements, which gives about two kinematic nodes per element in each direction, and the internal energy is discontinuous linear (Q1). Kinematic DOFs are the degrees of freedom of the velocity, counting every component. They are counted on a uniform mesh at each level, and adaptive refinement refines only part of the domain, so a run has fewer.

Code
MARBL, an arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) code, based on the high-order finite element methods of Dobrev et al. (2012), Anderson et al. (2018) and Anderson et al. (2015), with more detail in the MAPP report

The mechanism

Where the vorticity comes from

A shock that crosses the interface between a light and a heavy fluid leaves vorticity on it. On a flat interface the density gradient , which is normal to the interface, is parallel to the pressure gradient across the shock, and nothing happens. On a rippled interface the two are tilted against each other, and that misalignment is a source of vorticity . For an inviscid, compressible flow the vorticity equation is

Stretches of the interface with opposite tilt receive vorticity of opposite sign, which is what rolls the interface up into spikes and bubbles after the first shock.

The reshock is the same mechanism a second time. The shock reflects from the wall at the end of the domain and comes back through the heavy fluid toward the light one, so reverses while does not. On a given stretch of interface the baroclinic term changes sign, and the new vorticity can cancel what is already there or add to it, depending on the shape of the interface when the shock arrives. Either way it lands on an interface that is already distorted, and the mixing layer breaks down into fine-scale mixing.
