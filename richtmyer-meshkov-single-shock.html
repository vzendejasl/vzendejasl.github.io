2D and 3D calculations

Richtmyer–Meshkov instability with a single shock

A shock crosses a perturbed interface between two fluids and leaves vorticity on it. The interface rolls up into spikes and bubbles, and the mixing region between the two fluids grows. Where the vorticity comes from is explained at the bottom of this page.

The initial perturbation of the interface follows Groom and Thornber (2020) and Groom and Thornber (2023). The 3D calculation comes first below, then the 2D calculations.

Code
MARBL, an arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) code, based on the high-order finite element methods of Dobrev et al. (2012), Anderson et al. (2018) and Anderson et al. (2015), with more detail in the MAPP report
Elements
3D calculation: 192 × 128 × 128 Q2Q1 elements, ≈ 75.5 million kinematic DOFs
Release
The movies are from a presentation authorized for public release. LLNL release number:

These are high-order finite element (FEM) calculations, so the mesh is counted in elements. In Q2Q1 the velocity and the mesh are continuous quadratic (Q2) elements, which gives about two kinematic nodes per element in each direction, and the internal energy is discontinuous linear (Q1). Kinematic DOFs are the degrees of freedom of the velocity, counting every component.

The mechanism

Where the vorticity comes from

A shock that crosses the interface between a light and a heavy fluid leaves vorticity on it. On a flat interface the density gradient , which is normal to the interface, is parallel to the pressure gradient across the shock, and nothing happens. On a rippled interface the two are tilted against each other, and that misalignment is a source of vorticity . There is no vorticity before the shock arrives, so the baroclinic term of the vorticity equation is the whole story at first:

The other terms of the full vorticity equation only stretch and compress vorticity that is already there, so they are left out here. The size of the baroclinic term is , where is the angle between the two gradients, so it grows with the tilt of the interface.

Stretches of the interface with opposite tilt receive vorticity of opposite sign, which is what rolls the interface up into spikes and bubbles. The full vorticity equation, and what happens when a second shock hits the interface, are on the reshock page.
